# Design project problem statement

SI 684 - Design project problem statement
Helping University Students Move More During and Beyond Study Time
Group Member: Weichen Liu, Zhongguo He, Zhengbin Song, Qiuyuan Chen, Valen Hu
1. Health Problem and Importance
Our project aims to help undergraduate and graduate students who spend long hours studying become more physically active. We focus on two related problems: sitting for long periods without a break and struggling to exercise regularly. We want to support both short activity breaks during study time and planned exercise outside study time.
These behaviors matter for long-term health. The World Health Organization links higher amounts of sedentary time with health risks such as cardiovascular disease and type 2 diabetes. WHO recommends replacing some sitting time with physical activity. It also recommends 150–300 minutes of moderate-intensity aerobic activity per week, or an equivalent amount of vigorous activity. Short movement breaks and regular exercise can support each other, but they are not the same thing.
Our project focuses on health promotion, not treating a disease. We want to make movement easier to fit into student life and help users build habits they can maintain.
2. Target Population and Needs
Our target users are undergraduate and graduate students aged 18 or older who often sit for long periods while studying. We especially want to support students who would like to move more but do not have a regular exercise routine. Common settings include libraries, study rooms, and home study spaces.
We think possible barriers include forgetting to take breaks, not wanting to lose focus, and not having a clear exercise plan. Assignments and unexpected tasks may also interrupt their plans. Long-term health benefits may feel too far away to motivate action today. These are design assumptions that we would explore through interviews and testing.
The product should make activity easy to start and allow users to adjust their plans. Missing one activity should not make them feel that their earlier effort was wasted.
3. Target Behaviors
We want to support two specific behaviors. First, we want students to take more movement breaks during independent study. These could include a short walk, getting water, or another activity that fits their space and physical abilities. Our initial default would suggest about three minutes of movement after 45 minutes of studying. A related university student trial used a target of two-to-four-minute breaks every 30–45 minutes. Our setting draws on that range, but it is not a medical rule for everyone. Users would be able to adjust it.
Second, we want students to increase their weekly planned exercise, such as brisk walking, cycling, or another aerobic activity they enjoy. Users could start with small goals based on their current activity level. We would record light movement breaks separately from moderate-intensity exercise. Briefly getting up would not automatically count toward the weekly exercise goal.
4. Initial Intervention Ideas
We plan to design a system that works across a phone, computer, and optional smartwatch. The computer would support study-break reminders, while the phone would be used for exercise planning, progress tracking, and rewards. A smartwatch could provide simple reminders and help record activity.
We would use action planning and coping planning to help users decide when and how to be active, and adjust their plans when something gets in the way. For example, if an assignment interrupts a planned workout, users could reschedule it or choose a shorter activity. Previous research suggests that planning-based interventions can help reduce study-related sitting.
The system could also use device-recorded activity to reduce manual logging, while still allowing users to review or edit their records. A smartwatch would be optional, and users could still use timers and simple activity logs without one.
To make the experience more engaging, users could earn points from completed activities and use them to grow or customize a virtual pet. Progress would build over time instead of depending on a perfect streak, so missing one day would not erase previous effort. We would test whether this reward system actually helps users stay active.
5. Success Criteria
We would evaluate behavior change and usability separately. Check-in totals and pet levels would not be enough to show success.
For study breaks, we would measure movement breaks per study hour and estimated time spent sitting without a break. We would aim for more movement breaks and shorter sitting periods. Comparisons would account for total study time and identify whether records came from a device or the user.
For weekly exercise, we would measure completed moderate-intensity aerobic activity minutes and exercise plan completion. At least 150 minutes per week would be a long-term goal based on WHO guidance. For users starting at a lower level, steady improvement from their own baseline would also count as early progress.
For usability, we would check whether users can understand their records, follow their plans, and adjust reminders. We would also explore whether rewards feel encouraging or stressful, and whether check-ins reflect actual activity.
Early prototype testing would focus on usability and reactions to reminders and rewards. A later behavior study could compare activity before and during use. Positive feedback about the prototype alone would not prove that it improves health or reduces disease risk.
